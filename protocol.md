# Protocol

## Ambient artificial intelligence scribes for clinical documentation: effects on documentation time, after-hours work, clinician burnout, note quality and note errors

### A systematic review and meta-analysis

Author: Raphael Malikian. Healthcare founder and operator, clinical AI builder. Independent researcher, no industry affiliation.

Protocol version: 1.0, 22 October 2026.
Registration: prepared for PROSPERO. Registration number pending at the time of writing. No screening or data extraction for the review had started when the search was run on 22 October 2026, so this protocol can be registered before extraction begins.
Methods standard: Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.5 (2024). Reporting will follow PRISMA 2020. This review is Cochrane level in method. It is not a Cochrane review and will not be submitted to Cochrane.
Scope note: this is evidence synthesis and policy discussion only. It makes no clinical recommendation and no medication recommendation, and it gives no advice for the care of any individual patient.

## 1. Background

Clinicians spend a large share of the working day writing notes. Health systems have bought ambient artificial intelligence scribes at enterprise scale on the claim that the tools cut that work. An ambient scribe listens to a clinical encounter, with patient consent, and drafts a structured note that the clinician reviews, edits and signs.

The claim is easy to state and hard to test. A tool can cut typing time and add checking time in the same shift. It can shorten the note writing task and lengthen the note. It can lift mood at 30 days and do nothing at a year. It can also write things that were never said, or leave out things that were.

Earlier synthesis does not answer the question as buyers ask it. Zhao and colleagues (2025) pooled artificial intelligence tools for clinical note creation in general, 23 studies, and found a standardised mean difference of minus 0.71 for documentation burden with heterogeneity I squared 93.3 percent and study quality described as generally low. That review came out before the 2025 randomised trials. No published synthesis sets the time and burnout outcomes against note error outcomes in the same review. That gap is the reason for this review.

## Purpose

This review asks whether ambient AI scribes change documentation time, after-hours work, clinician burnout, note quality and note errors, and whether any gain holds across products, specialties, study designs and follow-up lengths. It also asks who funded the studies that report the gains.

Research questions:
- RQ1. What is the effect of ambient AI scribes on documentation time, measured from electronic health record audit logs or timed observation?
- RQ2. What is the effect on after-hours documentation time, often called pajama time?
- RQ3. What is the effect on clinician burnout, work exhaustion and professional fulfillment, measured on validated scales?
- RQ4. What is the effect on note quality, measured on a named instrument?
- RQ5. How often do ambient scribe notes contain omissions, hallucinations or fabrications, and wrong inclusions, per note audited?
- RQ6. Do effects differ by product, specialty, study design, vendor funding and follow-up length?

## 2. Objectives

To assess the effects of ambient AI scribes, compared with usual documentation, dictation or manual typing, on documentation time, after-hours work, clinician burnout, note quality and note errors in outpatient and inpatient care.

## 3. Eligibility criteria

### 3.1 Types of studies

Include:
- Randomised controlled trials, including cluster randomised and stepped wedge designs.
- Non-randomised studies with a concurrent comparison group, including controlled before and after studies and cohort studies with a comparator.
- Uncontrolled before and after studies and interrupted time series. These are synthesised apart from controlled studies.
- Note audit studies that grade a defined sample of ambient scribe notes for errors against the encounter record, the audio, or a human reference note, and report a denominator in notes.

Exclude from the synthesis:
- Qualitative studies, interviews and focus groups with no quantitative outcome from the list in section 4.
- Editorials, commentaries, letters with no original data, opinion pieces and news items.
- Protocols and trial registrations with no results. These are listed as ongoing studies.
- Systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews and meta-analyses. These are used for reference checking and for the discussion. They are not pooled.
- Case reports, single clinician anecdotes, and simulation studies with no real clinical encounter.
- Vendor white papers and marketing material with no peer reviewed or independently reported data.
- Conference abstracts with too little data to extract an outcome and a denominator. These are listed as awaiting classification where they look eligible in other respects.

### 3.2 Types of participants

Clinicians who document patient encounters in any specialty and any country: physicians, advanced practice clinicians such as nurse practitioners and physician assistants, and other documenting clinicians. Studies of mixed clinician groups are included and, where data allow, analysed by clinician type. Studies whose participants are only medical students, scribes employed as humans, patients, or administrators are excluded.

Settings: outpatient, inpatient, emergency and virtual care, primary and secondary care. Studies set in a classroom or a lab with simulated patients and no real encounter documentation are excluded from the main synthesis, except note audit studies that use standardised real encounter recordings and report error counts per note, which are included for RQ5 and flagged as standardised audits in extraction.

### 3.3 Types of intervention

Ambient AI scribe: software that captures a clinical encounter by listening, with consent, and generates a draft clinical note for clinician review and signature, with no dictation step by the clinician. Products named in the protocol concept include Microsoft DAX Copilot and Dragon Ambient eXperience, Nabla, Abridge, and Ambience Healthcare products. Equivalent ambient products are eligible on the same test: encounter capture plus draft note generation.

Exclude:
- Dictation and speech recognition tools that only transcribe what the clinician says.
- Tools where the clinician types or pastes text and a language model returns a summary, unless the study reports the ambient arm apart from other arms.
- Human medical scribes, in person or remote, with no AI note generation.
- Coding, billing, prior authorisation and inbox message tools that do not draft the encounter note, unless the study also reports an eligible ambient note outcome apart.
- General electronic health record changes rolled out at the same time as a scribe where the scribe effect cannot be separated. Such studies are recorded as excluded with that reason stated.

Length of use: no minimum. Follow-up length is extracted and used in subgroup analysis, under 90 days against 90 days or more.

### 3.4 Types of comparison

Usual documentation, manual typing, dictation, speech recognition, human scribe, no scribe, or the same clinicians in a pre-implementation period. Studies with no comparison are eligible only as note audit studies under section 3.1 or as uncontrolled before and after studies where a pre period is measured.

### 3.5 Report characteristics

Dates: 1 January 2019 to 22 October 2026 for the first search. Language: no limit at search. Non-English reports will be translated where a translation can be checked, and otherwise listed as awaiting classification. They will not be dropped without a record. Publication type: peer reviewed journal articles, published trial results, and preprints with original data. Preprints are flagged in extraction and removed in a sensitivity analysis if a peer reviewed version exists.

## 4. Outcome measures

### 4.1 Critical outcomes

1. Documentation time. Minutes in note per encounter, per note, per patient or per day, taken from electronic health record audit logs where the study gives them, or from timed observation. Time point groups: during implementation to 90 days, and over 90 days. Where a study reports several time points, the latest time point in each group is used in the main analysis.
2. After-hours documentation time. Minutes of documentation work outside scheduled hours per day or per week, from audit logs, or a study defined equivalent. The study definition is extracted word for word.
3. Burnout and work exhaustion. Scores on a validated scale, for example the Mini-Z, the Stanford Professional Fulfillment Index work exhaustion subscale, the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory, the Maslach Burnout Inventory, or a single item burnout measure that the study names. Scale, direction and range are extracted for each study. Single item measures are pooled apart from multi item scales in a sensitivity check where numbers allow.
4. Note errors, per note audited. Three types, kept apart at every stage:
   - Omissions: clinical content present in the encounter or reference record and absent from the draft note.
   - Hallucinations or fabrications: content in the draft note with no basis in the encounter or record.
   - Wrong inclusions: content that belongs to a different patient, a different encounter, or a template default carried in by error.
   Denominator: notes audited. Where a study reports errors per note as a count rather than notes with at least one error, that count is extracted as a rate and not mixed with proportions.

### 4.2 Important outcomes

5. Note quality. Score on a named instrument, for example the Physician Documentation Quality Instrument (PDQI-9), the QNOTE, or a study scale with stated items and a stated grader. Who graded the note, and whether the grader knew which tool wrote it, are extracted.
6. Professional fulfillment. Stanford Professional Fulfillment Index fulfillment subscale or an equivalent validated measure.
7. Time to note closure. Hours or days from encounter to signed note.
8. Patient experience. A named patient reported measure tied to the encounter where the study reports one.
9. Note length. Words or characters per note. Reported as a cost signal, since longer notes add reading work for the next clinician.

Outcomes with no data are reported as no data in the summary of findings table. They are not removed from the list because the result was empty or unhelpful.

## 6. Selection of studies

Two stages: title and abstract, then full text. Each stage is recorded in counts that feed the PRISMA 2020 flow: records identified per source, duplicates removed, records screened, records excluded at title and abstract, full texts sought, full texts not retrieved, full text exclusions with one reason each, studies included.

### LLM team in place of a human research team

A Cochrane review is normally written by a team: a search specialist, two screeners who work apart, two extractors, a statistician, and editors who check method. This review has one human author. In place of that team it uses a team of large language model roles, set up to work the way the human team works, with the same separations. The point is to come as close to a Cochrane review as one author can get, and to report, in numbers, how well the substitute team held up.

Roles, named to match the human jobs:

1. Information specialist. Builds a search string for each database, in that database syntax, runs it or marks it run pending access, and records string, date and hit count in the search log. No database is reported as searched without a dated run.
2. Screener A. Works through every title and abstract alone and records include, exclude or maybe, with a reason for each exclude.
3. Screener B. Does the same work in a separate pass, with a separate prompt, and does not see screener A calls until both passes are complete.
4. Adjudicator. A third pass. Takes every record where A and B disagree, reads title and abstract again, and settles the call with a logged reason. Items it cannot settle are flagged for the human author, by record ID.
5. Extractor A. Extracts each included study on the piloted form.
6. Extractor B. Extracts the same studies alone, in a separate pass. The two sheets are compared field by field. Every mismatch is logged and settled against the source document.
7. Risk of bias assessor pair. Two independent passes on RoB 2 for trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies, per outcome, with domain judgements and the text that backs each one. Disagreements go to the human author.
8. Statistician. Writes the meta-analysis code, runs it, and checks it a second way: a hand check of one pool against the extraction sheet, and a rerun after any correction. Code and data are posted with the review.
9. GRADE assessor. Drafts the GRADE rating per outcome with the reason for each downgrade or the reason none was made, against the downgrade points set in section 10. The human author signs each rating.
10. Methods editor. Checks the finished manuscript against PRISMA 2020, MECIR style conduct standards, and the Cochrane Handbook structure: abstract order, summary of findings placement, methods in seven parts, results in three parts, conclusions split into practice and research. Its checklist is filed with the manuscript.

Independence rules:
- A and B passes are run apart. Neither prompt contains the other pass calls, and the files are written apart before any comparison.
- Comparison happens only in a merge step, by code, which counts agreements and disagreements. The merge step makes no judgement of its own.
- The adjudicator sees both calls and both reasons, plus the record. It does not see which pass the human author prefers, since he has not recorded one.
- Prompts, model name and version, and dates for each role sit in llm-team-log.md. A prompt changed mid-task is a new version in the log, and work done under the old version is named.

Human oversight:
Raphael Malikian is principal investigator and accountable human author. He set the review question and the eligibility rules in this protocol. He settles every item the adjudicator flags, every risk of bias disagreement, and every GRADE rating, and he checks every extracted number that enters a pool against the source document before he signs off the final numbers. No number in the finished review rests on a model pass alone.

Disclosure, RAISE 2025:
This use follows the 2025 RAISE statement on responsible AI use in evidence synthesis, from Cochrane, Campbell, JBI and the Collaboration for Environmental Evidence. Tool, version, dates, purpose and checks are reported here and in the finished review. Any journal or conference AI use statement will carry the same facts: a team of language model roles did the team tasks, under the separations above, and the named human author is accountable for every judgement.

Mechanics reported as results:
The workings of the team are data, and the finished review reports them in its methods and results: inter-screener agreement rate and Cohen kappa at title and abstract, the count and type of screener disagreements, adjudication outcomes split into A upheld, B upheld, and sent to the author, full text agreement in the same form, and the extraction discrepancy rate per field. The log that holds these counts is llm-team-log.md. Reporting them is part of the contribution of this review: a reader can judge the substitute team on its numbers, the same way they judge the clinical studies.

For this first pass on 22 October 2026, title and abstract calls in screening.csv are the first pass set out above. Records called include or maybe go to full text retrieval. No record is finally included in the review until its full text has been read and checked.

## 7. Data extraction

Two independent extractor passes, A and B, as set out in the LLM team section, using the piloted form in extraction-form.md. The sheets are compared field by field by code. Each mismatch is logged with its field name, settled against the source document, and counted toward the extraction discrepancy rate in llm-team-log.md. The human author checks every number that will enter a pool. The form was drafted from the outcomes in section 4 and the bias concerns in section 8 before screening began. Items: study identity, country, setting, specialty mix, design, clinician count and type, encounter or note count, product name and version, consent model, how the draft note was reviewed, comparator, follow-up length, each outcome with measure, time point, value and spread, note audit method and grader, funding source, vendor role in design, data handling and writing, registration number, and conflicts stated by the study authors.

Study authors will be contacted for missing outcome data where an address is given, once, with a two week window. Replies and non-replies are logged.

## 8. Risk of bias

RoB 2 for randomised trials, five domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The effect of interest is assignment to the scribe.

ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies, seven domains, in the templates in rob-forms.md. Confounding set in advance: baseline documentation load, specialty mix, concurrent electronic health record changes, and self-selection of keen users into scribe groups. Co-interventions set in advance: documentation training, template changes and staffing changes in the same period.

Two independent assessor passes make each judgement, as set out in the LLM team section. Agreements stand. Disagreements, with domain and outcome named, go to the human author for settlement, and the count is logged in llm-team-log.md.
Judgements are made per outcome, not per study in general, where the data allow. A study can be sound on audit log time and weak on a self reported burnout score gathered from volunteers. The templates force that split.

## 9. Synthesis

Analysis in R, written and checked by the statistician role as set out in the LLM team section, and signed off by the human author. Random effects model, inverse variance, with tau squared and I squared reported for every pool, and a prediction interval where ten or more studies pool.

- Mean difference where studies report the same measure on the same basis, for example minutes per note.
- Standardised mean difference where burnout, exhaustion, fulfillment and task load scales differ across studies. Direction is set so a lower score means less burnout for every scale before pooling, and the change is logged per study.
- Before and after studies are pooled apart from controlled studies. A combined pool is shown only as a sensitivity check, labelled as such.
- Error outcomes are pooled as proportions of notes with at least one error of that type, per study, with omissions, hallucinations or fabrications, and wrong inclusions kept apart in separate pools. Error counts per note are pooled as rates apart from proportions.
- Where pooling would mix unlike measures, no pool is run. The result is reported with SWiM style structured narrative synthesis, with effect direction, sample size and risk of bias shown per study.

Heterogeneity is expected. Zhao 2025 reported I squared 93.3 percent for documentation time. A high I squared is a finding to explain through the subgroups below, not a reason to hide the pool.

### Subgroup analyses, set in advance
- Product: DAX or Dragon Ambient eXperience, Nabla, Abridge, Ambience, other or mixed.
- Specialty: primary care against other specialties, and mixed specialty studies apart.
- Study design: randomised against non-randomised controlled against uncontrolled before and after.
- Vendor funding and vendor authorship: vendor funded or vendor staffed against independent.
- Follow-up: under 90 days against 90 days or more.
- Clinician type where data allow.

Subgroup claims rest on a test for interaction. A result inside one subgroup reaching a threshold on its own is not a subgroup finding.

### Sensitivity analyses, set in advance
- Controlled studies only.
- Studies at low or moderate overall risk of bias only.
- Leave one product out, run in turn.
- Preprints removed.
- Single item burnout measures removed.

### Reporting bias
Funnel plot and Egger test only where ten or more studies pool. Registered studies are compared with their published outcomes where a registration can be found. Small study effects are discussed as a pattern, given how much of this field is vendor funded pilot work at single sites.

## 10. Certainty of the evidence

GRADE, per outcome, for a summary of findings table with at most seven outcomes. Downgrade domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Expected pressure points, stated now: self-selected users push risk of bias down a level in most before and after work; short follow-up makes 30 day burnout gains indirect for any lasting claim; wide intervals from small pilot samples hurt precision. Upgrading is not expected for any outcome.
