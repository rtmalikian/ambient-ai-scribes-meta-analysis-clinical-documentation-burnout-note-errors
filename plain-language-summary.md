# What we found, in plain words
## Ambient AI scribes: the programs that listen to your appointment and write the doctor's notes

### What is an AI scribe?

When you see a doctor, someone has to write up what was said. That writing takes a long time, and a lot of it gets done at home in the evening. An AI scribe is a program that listens to the appointment, with the patient's permission, and writes a first draft of the note. The doctor reads the draft, fixes it, and signs it.

Hospitals and clinics spent about $600 million on these programs in 2025. The sales pitch is simple: less typing, less work at home, less burnout. We wanted to know if the research backs that up.

### What we did

We searched the medical literature and found 1,049 reports. After removing copies and studies that did not fit, 74 studies were left. Then we combined their results, checked each study for weak spots, and graded how sure we can be about each answer.

One difference from a normal research team: this review was run by one researcher working with a team of AI assistants, each doing a separate job, checking each other's work. Every disagreement between them was written down. The human researcher made the final calls and signed off every number.

### What we found

**Doctors spend less time writing notes.** Across the studies, note-writing time fell by about a fifth on average. But the results varied a lot from place to place. Some clinics saved a good deal of time. The numbers say another clinic could save little or nothing.

**Evening work at home might fall, but nobody has proved it.** The studies point to a bit less work after hours, yet not one of them had a proper comparison group. If your boss tells you this program will give you your evenings back, the honest answer is: maybe, and it has not been tested properly.

**Burnout scores improved in the best studies.** Two proper trials, where doctors were assigned by chance to use a scribe or not, found that work exhaustion eased. That is the strongest result in the review. It is still only two trials, and they ran for one to three months.

**The notes read better, and they still contain mistakes.** Notes written with a scribe scored higher on quality rating scales. At the same time, the biggest careful check of error types found that out of every 100 AI-drafted notes, about 18 missed something that was said, about 12 included something that was never said, and about 9 included something that belonged in a different note. A note can read well and still be wrong. That is why the doctor still has to read every line.

**The studies themselves are weak.** Most were small, most ran for one to three months, and in most of them the doctors who volunteered to try the new tool were compared with everyone else. People who volunteer for a tool they want tend to report that it helped. Out of 74 studies, 55 had serious weak spots of this kind.

### The bottom line

AI scribes probably do save doctors some writing time, and they may ease exhaustion. They also make mistakes that a person has to catch. The time saved is real but uneven, the evening-work claim is unproven, and almost everything we know comes from short, small studies.

If a clinic buys one of these programs, the research says to keep measuring after it is installed: time, evening work, and errors, all three. Any seller who quotes only the time saving is telling you half of what the studies found.

### One more thing

No result here says which product to buy. The studies did not show one brand beating another. And nothing in this review is medical advice. It is a summary of published research about office software, written so patients and staff can see what the evidence actually says.
